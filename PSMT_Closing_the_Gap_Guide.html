Writing your Closing the Gap report

A working guide to the Year 10 problem-solving and modelling task (FIA4, Univariate and Bivariate Data). Statement to evaluate: Australia is on track to close the gap in life expectancy between First Nations and non-Indigenous Australians by 2031.

4Formulate 7Solve 5Evaluate 4Communicate

Out of 20. Solve is the biggest share, but Evaluate and Formulate together are worth nearly half.

  • Wk 2 Fri: Formulate complete
  • Wk 3 Fri: Solve complete
  • Wk 4 Fri: Evaluation and full draft
  • Wk 5 Fri: Final copy
  • Wk 6–7: 12-slide oral presentation

How to use this guide. The examples show the standard to aim for. Rewrite everything in your own words and make your own modelling decisions. The task is individual, and your teacher uses plagiarism-detection software and class cross-marking. Your decisions and reasoning are what get marked.

Watch out

The appendix is not marked, so anything the marker needs to see must be in the main report. Put long working in the appendix and the result and meaning in the body.

Formulate (4 marks)

Turn the real-world question into a mathematical one. The top band asks for justified assumptions, justified observations and a justified mathematical translation.

Observations

An observation is something you notice from the task or the data. Give the source, then say why it matters for your model. Use a repeating pattern so every observation is justified:

Example wording
It is observed that the target is to close the life expectancy gap by 2031. It is justified by the fact that "on track" can only be judged against a deadline, so the model needs to predict the gap in 2031 (task sheet).
It is observed that both data sets are reported for the same periods. It is justified by the fact that matching periods let me subtract corresponding values to find the gap for each period.
It is observed that the data comes from the Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS), which estimates life expectancy from registered deaths and Census population counts, and is published by the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (AIHW). It is justified by the sources named in the spreadsheets, so I treat it as reliable but not exact.
Your turn

Find an observation about the data itself (for example, which group has the larger gap, or how many time points you have) and write it with "It is observed that" and "It is justified by".

Assumptions

An assumption is something you accept as true to make the model workable. Each needs a reason and an effect on the model. Aim for a table like this, in your own words:

Watch out

You will come back to every assumption in Evaluate. The QCAA's top sample lost its only mark because it never judged its results against its assumptions. Write assumptions you can honestly test later.

Mathematical translation

This is where the real-world question becomes variables, a model and a decision rule. Define each of these:

  • Variables: t = time (year, or years since your first data point) and G = gap in years (non-Indigenous life expectancy minus Indigenous life expectancy).
  • Model: G = mt + c, a line of best fit through the (t, G) points.
  • Meaning of the slope m: negative means the gap is closing, near zero means constant, positive means growing. These match the three outcomes in the task sheet.
  • Target in maths: G = 0 in 2031.
  • Decision rule: state exactly what "on track" means. For example: the model predicts a gap of 0 by 2031, or the slope is at least as steep as the rate needed (latest gap divided by years remaining).
Example wording
I translate "close the gap" as G = 0. The rate of change needed from the latest data point (2021) to reach this in 2031 is the latest gap divided by 10 years. I will compare this with the slope of my model. If the modelled slope is much smaller in size, the gap is not closing quickly enough to be on track.

Verify before you use

The 2015–17 and 2020–22 rows come from your spreadsheets. I gathered the 2005–07 and 2010–12 rows from search results, so check them against the ABS tables yourself and cite the ABS, not this guide. Sources differ slightly on the 2005–07 gap (the Productivity Commission uses 11.4 and 9.6).

Comparability: a strength of your Evaluate section

  • Method change. The ABS changed its estimation method for 2020–22 and recalculated 2015–17 with it. The recalculated 2015–17 gaps are 8.7 and 8.0, not 8.6 and 7.8. Check the ABS 2020–22 release, then say which version you use and why.
  • Population change. The ABS cautioned that comparisons between older periods should be read carefully because of how the population was counted.
  • Rounding. Subtracting rounded life expectancies sometimes differs by 0.1 from the published gap. Use the published gap and say so.
  • Estimates, not measurements. Life expectancy is calculated from death registrations and Census counts, and adjusted for how Indigenous status is recorded. The 2024 file gives 95% confidence intervals you can use to discuss uncertainty.

The topic is about real people and communities. Write respectfully, use the terms from the task sheet (First Nations, non-Indigenous), describe what the numbers show without blame or stereotypes, and only claim causes you can support with a source.

Solve (7 marks)

The top band needs accurate maths, efficient use of technology and a complete solution, meaning you answer the question you set.

  1. Calculate the gap for each period: gap = non-Indigenous minus Indigenous, for males and for females separately.
  2. Choose your time variable. Midpoint years (2006, 2011, 2016, 2021) or years since 2006 (0, 5, 10, 15). The second gives an intercept you can interpret.
  3. Draw a scatterplot of gap against time for each sex. Title it, label the axes with units, and say what you see (direction, strength, any outlier).
  4. Fit a line of best fit with technology and report the equation, r and R². See the tool notes below.
  5. Interpret the model in context. Slope: "the gap changes by about … years each year". Intercept: "the modelled gap in 2006 was …".
  6. Predict. Use the line to find the gap in 2031, and the year the line reaches 0.
  7. Compare with the rate needed. Needed rate = latest gap ÷ (2031 − 2021). Is your slope close to it?
  8. Answer the question. State clearly, using your decision rule, whether Australia is on track.
Technology: Excel

Put the years in column A and the gaps in column B. Then use:

  • =SLOPE(B2:B5, A2:A5) and =INTERCEPT(B2:B5, A2:A5)
  • =CORREL(A2:A5, B2:B5) for r, and =RSQ(B2:B5, A2:A5) for R²
  • =FORECAST.LINEAR(2031, B2:B5, A2:A5) for the predicted gap in 2031
  • Or insert a scatter chart, add a linear trendline, and tick "display equation" and "display R-squared".

Screenshot the sheet and chart for the appendix.

Technology: Desmos

Add a table with columns x₁ and y₁ and enter your points. In a new line type y₁ ~ mx₁ + b. Desmos shows the best-fit values of m and b, and r and R² below. Add the line x = 2031 to mark the target year.

Technology: TI-Nspire CX

In a Lists and Spreadsheet page, enter the time values in one column and the gaps in another, and name the columns. Then go to Menu, Statistics, Stat Calculations, Linear Regression (mx+b). Choose your X and Y lists. The output includes m, b, r and R².

  • Slope m = −25.25 ÷ 125 = −0.202 years per year.
  • Intercept c = 9.875 − (−0.202)(7.5) = 11.39, so the model is G = 11.39 − 0.202x.
  • Correlation r ≈ −0.926, so R² ≈ 0.858: a strong negative linear relationship.
  • Prediction for 2031 (x = 25): G = 11.39 − 0.202 × 25 = 6.34 years.
  • Gap reaches 0 when x = 11.39 ÷ 0.202 ≈ 56.4, which is about the year 2062.
  • Rate needed from 2021 to hit 0 by 2031: 8.8 ÷ 10 = 0.88 years per year. The model's slope is 0.202, so the gap would need to close about 4.4 times faster.
Example wording for interpreting
The slope of −0.202 means the model predicts the male gap shrinks by about 0.2 years each year. Closing the 8.8-year gap by 2031 would need about 0.88 years each year, so the trend is moving in the right direction but too slowly to reach 0 by 2031.

What the presets teach

Load "Males, two points only". The line now slopes upwards and predicts a larger gap in 2031, the opposite of the four-point result. A line through two points always fits perfectly (r = 1 or −1), so r tells you nothing. This is a good limitation to explain in Evaluate, and a reason to source more data.

Evaluate and verify (5 marks)

The top band needs verified results, justified statements about reasonableness that consider your assumptions and your observations, and justified strengths and limitations.

Verify your results

  • Check by two methods. Recompute the slope by hand and with technology and show they agree.
  • Check the fit. Compare each predicted value with the actual one. Residuals should be small and roughly balanced between positive and negative.
  • Test sensitivity. Re-run the model with the revised 2015–17 value, or without one point, and report how much the prediction changes.
  • Check the sign and size. Does a negative slope match the falling gaps in the table? Is a 2031 prediction between the latest gap and 0 sensible?

Justify the reasonableness of your answer

Go back through your assumptions and observations one by one and say whether each held up.

Example wording
Assumption: linear trend. The gap fell quickly from 2006 to 2016 but was almost flat from 2016 to 2021, so a straight line may overstate recent progress. My r value of −0.93 supports a linear fit, but with four points this is weak evidence. This makes the 2031 prediction an optimistic estimate.
Observation: estimates, not exact values. The 2024 file gives 95% confidence intervals, and a change of 0.2 years between periods is small compared with this uncertainty. So I cannot be sure the gap changed at all between 2016 and 2021.

Strengths and limitations

Each statement needs an effect, and ideally an improvement.

Strengths

  • Uses official ABS data that is consistent for both groups, so the gap is a fair comparison.
  • The model is simple to interpret: slope = years of gap closed per year.
  • Four time points, spanning 15 years, show a long-term pattern, not just one change.
  • Comparing the modelled slope with the rate needed gives a clear, testable answer.

Limitations

  • Only four points, so r and the line are fragile. A new data point could change the prediction a lot.
  • 2031 is 10 years beyond the data (extrapolation), so the prediction is uncertain.
  • The method changed between releases, so older and newer points may not be fully comparable.
  • The model ignores causes such as health programs and population changes.
Example wording
A limitation is that the model uses only four data points. This means a single revised estimate could noticeably change the slope. To improve it, I could add data for more periods, or use the confidence intervals to give a range for the prediction.

Communicate (4 marks)

Use correct mathematical language, organise the report logically, justify your decisions, and make sure it can be read without the task sheet. The oral presentation counts here, and you cannot earn all four marks without a complete one.

OutlierA point far from the pattern. Say whether you found one and what you did.

Correlation does not mean causation. A trend in the gap does not on its own explain why the gap changed.

Presentation of the report

  • Number and title every table and figure, label axes with units, refer to each in the text, and put the source under it.
  • Start the report with the context and the statement, so a reader with no task sheet knows what you are doing.
  • Define "on track" once, early, and use the same words each time.
  • Give each section a clear job and keep to the scaffold headings.
  • Use in-text citations such as (AIHW, 2024) that match your reference list.

What the QCAA samples show

The QCAA publishes annotated sample responses to its senior Maths Methods problem-solving task. They are Year 11 and 12 work, but they use the same four criteria as your task, so they show what markers reward.

High sample (19/20)

  • The introduction states the task, the approach and the finding.
  • Each observation cites its source and explains how the data was collected.
  • Assumptions are numbered, each with a reason and its effect on the model.
  • Models were compared with a measurable test, not just by eye.
  • Code and repeated calculations are in the appendix.
  • Strengths and limitations each give an effect and a way to improve.
  • It lost one mark: its evaluation did not consider its assumptions.

Mid sample (12/20)

  • Showed a data table with no source.
  • Listed strengths and limitations without explaining their effect.
  • Did not fully connect the model's results back to the question.
  • Had a very brief conclusion.
  • No in-text referencing or reference list.

The pattern: the stronger report justifies, cites and links every decision. The weaker report states things without backing them up.
